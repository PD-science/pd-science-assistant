## One-sentence value

This review synthesizes human clinical evidence and animal models supporting an oral-gut-brain axis in which oral and gut dysbiosis may promote systemic inflammation and neuroinflammation, with potential relevance to Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, metabolic disease, and microbiome-targeted therapy.

## Abstract translation

**Objectives:** Oral health is inseparably linked with systemic health, as highlighted by the emerging oral-gut-brain axis and other established axes. Growing evidence supports a complex oral-gut-brain axis connecting the oral and gut microbiomes with the central nervous system. Disruptions of this axis, manifesting as oral and gut dysbiosis or microbial imbalance, can trigger oral and systemic inflammation as well as neuroinflammation, contributing to diseases such as Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease.

**Materials and methods:** The authors summarize mechanistic pathways of the oral-gut-brain axis, key evidence from human clinical and animal studies, and how the oral microbiome modulates human health and disease.

**Results:** Periodontal disease is associated with increased presence of oral pathogens in diseased tissues throughout the human body. Preclinical models reproduce these findings. Experimental periodontal infection induces dysbiosis linked to activation of inflammatory pathways that promote disease phenotypes. Novel therapeutic approaches, including the probiotic bacteriocin nisin, are increasingly recognized as targeted microbiome therapy at multiple inflection points across the axis. Nisin restores microbial balance, reduces inflammation, inhibits end-organ pathology, prevents periodontal bone loss, and reduces brain amyloid/tau accumulation and cytokine expression.

**Conclusions:** These findings highlight the complexity of the oral-gut-brain axis and the possibility of modulating this axis using bacteriocin-based approaches.

**Clinical relevance:** Future probiotic or antimicrobial strategies aimed at improving neuroinflammatory and metabolic diseases through microbiome-targeted therapy have clinical promise.

## Core framework

The oral-gut-brain axis is a bidirectional communication network linking the oral microbiome, gut microbiome, and central nervous system. The review emphasizes four communication routes:

1. Neural routes, including vagal and trigeminal pathways.
2. Circulatory routes, including microbial components and inflammatory mediators entering systemic circulation.
3. Immune routes, including cytokine-driven systemic inflammation and neuroinflammation.
4. Endocrine/metabolic routes, including short-chain fatty acids and other microbial metabolites.

The central mechanistic motif is "leaky barriers":

- leaky gingival barrier in periodontal disease;
- leaky gut barrier in intestinal dysbiosis;
- altered blood-brain barrier permeability under inflammatory and oxidative stress.

## Evidence summary

### Oral-brain axis

Human studies associate periodontal disease, tooth loss, dental plaque, and gum bleeding with cognitive decline, dementia risk, and Alzheimer's disease-related biomarkers. Some studies detect periodontal pathogens, lipopolysaccharide, or gingipain-related signatures in Alzheimer's disease brain tissue or cerebrospinal fluid. However, the review also notes inconsistencies: some epidemiological links may be confounded by depression, traumatic brain injury, hyperlipidemia, or other comorbidities.

Animal models provide stronger mechanistic support. Oral infection with periodontal pathogens such as Porphyromonas gingivalis can lead to brain translocation, microglial activation, amyloid beta accumulation, tau-related changes, and inflammatory cytokine induction. Nisin treatment mitigated periodontal-disease-induced brain microbiome dysbiosis and reduced IL-1beta, IL-6, TNF-alpha, amyloid beta 42, and tau in mouse models.

### Gut-brain axis

The review summarizes human and animal evidence connecting gut dysbiosis with Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease, depression, autism spectrum disorder, sleep, pain processing, and brain tumor immunotherapy response. In Parkinson's disease, cited studies report increased Lactobacillus and reduced Bacteroides fragilis and C. coccoides in patients compared with controls, as well as gut microbiome signatures linked to disease progression and early signs.

Mouse models suggest potential causality: fecal microbiota transplantation from Parkinson's disease patients into mice can induce gut dysbiosis, intestinal barrier disruption, motor dysfunction, inflammation, and dopaminergic/midbrain-related changes. Germ-free mouse systems further support a role for microbial exposure in blood-brain barrier permeability, pain sensitivity, neurodevelopment, stress response, and behavior.

### Oral-gut axis

The oral microbiome can influence gut disease and colorectal cancer. Fusobacterium nucleatum is highlighted in colorectal cancer, including mechanisms involving Fap2 binding to Gal-GalNAc, IL-8 and CXCL1 activation, immune cell mobilization, and chemoresistance through TLR4/MYD88-autophagy pathways. In inflammatory bowel disease, salivary and fecal dysbiosis patterns and meta-analyses support links between IBD and periodontal disease.

Mouse models show that oral pathogens can alter gut microbiota, increase endotoxin, disrupt tight junction-related intestinal barrier markers, and promote Th17/IL-1beta-driven gut inflammation.

## Therapeutic angle: nisin

The review highlights nisin, a bacteriocin produced by Lactococcus lactis, as a candidate microbiome-targeted therapy. Nisin is described as an antimicrobial peptide with activity against multiple oral pathogens and biofilms. In preclinical models, nisin:

- shifts oral, gut, liver, and brain microbiomes toward healthier profiles;
- reduces periodontal inflammation and bone loss;
- reduces small intestinal inflammation and preserves villus architecture;
- attenuates liver steatosis, oxidative stress, and dysbiosis in NAFLD-related models;
- suppresses brain IL-1beta, IL-6, TNF-alpha, amyloid beta 42, and tau after periodontal-disease-induced dysbiosis.

The authors also discuss delivery strategies, including nisin-producing Lactococcus lactis and solid lipid nanoparticle formulations.

## Limitations and cautions

- Much of the mechanistic evidence comes from preclinical models.
- Human associations do not prove causality.
- Oral-gut-brain communication is likely influenced by age, diet, stress, lifestyle, systemic disease, medication, genetics, and environment.
- Microbiome-targeted therapies such as nisin remain promising but require clinical trials for neuroinflammatory and metabolic disease applications.
